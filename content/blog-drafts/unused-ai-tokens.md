# What to Do With Unused AI Tokens: 7 Ways to Help Research

If you have unused AI tokens or spare AI usage in a subscription, put that capacity toward a question that someone else needs answered. You could find original sources, compare competing claims, organize public data, or check an existing research result. A contribution becomes useful when another person can inspect the evidence and continue the work.

Start with one bounded task. For example, compare the published methods of three public datasets, then record their coverage, update dates, and limitations. That leaves a usable research artifact after the AI session ends.

## What is unused AI usage?

Unused AI usage is capacity you still have available in an AI tool. You might also call it spare AI allowance, remaining usage, or unused AI tokens. It could mean room under a subscription’s limits, remaining API credits, or available capacity on a local model.

Those resources have different costs and controls. Check your own plan’s usage, reset schedule, and billing settings before deciding what to contribute.

On LLM.Giving, you use your own AI tools and share the resulting research. The site does not connect to your subscription or take custody of your API keys. You choose the work and run it yourself. The [participation guide](/about) explains the workflow.

## 1. Build a source map for a focused question

A source map helps the next researcher find the material worth reading. Choose a question with clear boundaries, such as which public datasets could support a local heat-mapping project.

Ask your AI to identify original datasets, methodology documents, and relevant research papers. Open the sources yourself and record who published each one, when it was updated, what it covers, and why it matters. Include a direct link to the relevant document or dataset.

The deliverable is a short annotated bibliography. Explain what each source contributes and which part of the research question it helps answer. That gives the next volunteer a reason to read it.

## 2. Turn a broad question into a workable research plan

A question such as “How can our community improve digital accessibility?” needs narrower investigations before anyone can answer it usefully.

Use AI to propose a sequence: identify the intended users, inventory existing services, compare accessibility guidance, and define a small evaluation. For each task, specify the expected output and what evidence would make it complete. Make dependencies explicit so volunteers know which work can begin now.

On LLM.Giving, approved topics begin with a planning task. Read that task’s current model requirements and use its supplied prompt and output format. The plan can then guide research, synthesis, and independent review.

## 3. Compare public information in a consistent table

Comparison work is useful when several sources describe the same thing differently. For a hypothetical community mapping project, you might compare open mapping tools by supported formats, documentation, license, and maintenance activity.

Choose the columns before collecting the data. Ask the AI to attach a source to every factual entry and to leave a field empty when it cannot establish the answer. Then check the entries against the original pages.

Include the date you checked changing information. A comparison becomes easier to maintain when a later contributor can see exactly where each entry came from.

## 4. Check a claim against its cited evidence

Take one consequential claim from a research result and follow its citation. Does the source discuss the same population, place, time period, and outcome? Does it support the strength of the wording?

AI can help identify the relevant passages and compare the claim with the source. Your contribution should show the passage you checked, explain the match or mismatch, and propose a correction when needed.

Suppose a report says a program improved access nationwide, while its source describes one pilot location. A useful correction would retain the pilot finding and narrow the geographic claim.

## 5. Document a public dataset so others can use it

A dataset can be hard to evaluate without a clear explanation of its fields, units, missing values, and coverage. Use AI to help read the documentation and draft a compact data dictionary.

Work from public data that you are permitted to use. Check a small sample against the original schema, preserve the source values, and document any transformations. If you identify inconsistent dates or units, show the rule you used to normalize them.

The output might be a data dictionary, an issue log, or a reproducible cleaning script. Keep personal or confidential records out of a public research contribution.

## 6. Explain a technical result for a specific reader

A community group may need a clear explanation of a technical report before it can decide what to investigate next. Ask your AI to draft an explanation for that audience, preserving the original result’s scope and uncertainty.

Define unfamiliar terms, connect each important claim to its source, and distinguish findings from proposed next steps. Check that the explanation has not turned an association into a causal claim or a preliminary result into settled guidance.

A useful deliverable could be a one-page briefing with a short glossary and links to the underlying material.

## 7. Review someone else’s completed work

Review helps a shared project catch mistakes and identify the next useful investigation. Choose work you did not produce, read the research question, and assess whether the submitted result answers it.

Check citations, calculations, omitted alternatives, and whether the stated conclusion follows from the evidence. Keep the review specific: name the claim, show the issue, and describe the change needed. Use a fresh AI run and follow the project’s independence requirements.

A result that holds up after careful checking is useful too. Explain what you examined so the next reader can understand the scope of the review.

## Make your first contribution manageable

Pick an output you can finish and inspect in the time you have. Reserve enough capacity to review the result and return it in the required format. If the work cannot be completed, report the actual progress and remaining steps rather than marking it finished.

Keep paid overages under your control. Use only resources you are authorized to use, and respect the rules of the project receiving your contribution. Spare capacity is an opportunity to help; there is no need to exhaust an allowance to make the work worthwhile.

If you prefer hands-on classification tasks, [Zooniverse’s volunteer guide](https://www.zooniverse.org/get-involved/volunteer) describes another route into research, with no minimum time commitment. Follow each project’s instructions about the use of AI.

To contribute your own AI-assisted research, [explore LLM.Giving’s research requests](/research), open a project, and read its available tasks. Choose a deliverable whose evidence you can check and whose result another contributor can build on.

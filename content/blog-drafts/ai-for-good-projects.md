# AI for Good Projects You Can Contribute to From Home

AI for good projects give you a way to apply technical skills, research time, or careful review to a public-interest problem. To find a useful fit, start with the contribution you can make: investigate a question, analyze data, review model outputs, or help a team explain its work.

You do not need to train a model to participate. Some routes suit experienced data scientists; others begin with reading instructions and checking a small piece of evidence.

## Choose a contribution that matches your resources

| Your starting point | A route to explore | What to check first |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spare AI usage and an interest in research | LLM.Giving research tasks | The task objective, completion criteria, and model requirements |
| Data science or quantitative experience | DataKind volunteering | Skills, application process, and project commitment |
| Research, communication, or policy interests | AI for Good Foundation volunteering | The requirements of the particular role |
| Interest in hands-on citizen science | Zooniverse projects | The tutorial and how AI is used in that project |

Choose work whose purpose and requested output you understand. A small contribution with a clear recipient is a useful place to begin.

## Contribute AI-assisted research with LLM.Giving

LLM.Giving is built around shared research questions. Volunteers use their own AI tools to investigate a task, gather evidence, and return a structured result. You can take part without a public account, and the site does not connect to your AI subscription.

Put unused AI usage toward a research task with an output you can inspect. You could compare public datasets, check the evidence behind a claim, synthesize earlier findings, or independently review a completed investigation. Reserve enough capacity to check the result and finish the submission.

For an illustrative community project, imagine a group comparing ways to make a public information service more accessible. A useful task could identify relevant guidance and document which recommendations apply to the group’s actual materials. The output would include source links, an explanation of the comparison, and questions for later testing.

Start by [browsing research requests](/research). Read the brief before choosing a ready task, and follow the supplied prompt and required return format. If you want to propose a question, [submit a research request](/submit); new topics are reviewed before becoming public.

## Apply data skills through DataKind

[DataKind’s volunteer program](https://www.datakind.org/join-us/volunteer/) connects quantitative experts with social-impact work. Its volunteer page describes remote participation, a profile-based application process, and projects involving issues such as poverty, healthcare access, and climate change.

This is a route for people who want to contribute relevant professional skills in a team setting. A particular project may call for experience in analysis, engineering, statistics, or another discipline. Read the role requirements and expected commitment before applying.

To begin, follow the current volunteer page to create a profile, describe your skills accurately, and look for opportunities that fit your experience. Project selection and team onboarding matter here, so plan for a different process from picking up a short open research task.

## Explore research and communication roles at AI for Good Foundation

The [AI for Good Foundation volunteer page](https://ai4good.org/get-involved/volunteer/) describes opportunities spanning research, sustainable-development policy, writing, and outreach. Individual roles have different eligibility and time requirements.

This route may suit someone who wants to support an organization’s broader work rather than contribute model runs alone. Read the complete description of a role and consider the deliverable you could maintain consistently. Writing experience, domain knowledge, or an ability to explain technical work can be relevant contributions.

If you need a role you can do from home, confirm that arrangement before applying through the organization’s stated process. Ask about permission before using personal AI tools with project materials, especially when a role involves internal documents or information about other people.

## Join citizen-science projects that combine people and AI

[Zooniverse](https://www.zooniverse.org/get-involved/volunteer) offers research participation through tasks such as classifying project data. Its volunteer guide states that there is no minimum time commitment. That makes it an option for someone who wants to begin with a small contribution.

AI involvement differs by project. Zooniverse’s [AI ethics framework](https://www.zooniverse.org/about/ai-ethics) describes several arrangements: machines may pre-sort data, volunteer classifications may help train models, or people may check machine-generated classifications. Other projects have no AI component.

Read the project’s About page and tutorial to understand the role of your work. Follow the requested classification method; bring a question to the research team if you are unsure whether outside AI assistance is appropriate.

## Five project ideas for a community group

If you already work with a nonprofit, library, or local group, start by asking what information would help it make a decision. Discuss a project shape that meets that need:

- **Public-source evidence map:** identify original sources relevant to a specific community question, with dates and scope notes.
- **Accessibility review:** compare a group’s public materials against an agreed checklist, then have people who use those materials test the proposed improvements.
- **Open-data documentation:** explain the fields, units, coverage, and limitations of a public dataset that a group wants to use.
- **Research translation:** turn an approved technical report into a plain-language briefing while retaining its sources and important qualifications.
- **Independent claim review:** examine a small set of consequential claims and document whether their cited sources support them.

Agree on the output and its intended user before beginning. For example, an accessibility checklist should help someone review a particular document or service. A dataset guide should explain enough for the next analyst to use the data correctly.

## Decide what useful progress will look like

Before committing, answer three questions: who will use the result, what decision or task will it support, and who will review it? These questions help keep the work connected to an actual need.

Choose a small first deliverable and get feedback before expanding it. Preserve source links and document any important assumptions so another volunteer can continue the work. Keep sensitive information within the receiving organization’s approved tools and permissions.

If you already have AI access and want a research task, [explore LLM.Giving](/research). Choose a question you care about, read what the next task requires, and contribute a result that another person can check.

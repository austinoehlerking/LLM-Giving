# Citizen Science With AI: Help Research Without a PhD

Citizen science gives people outside a professional research team a way to contribute to scientific work. AI can support that participation by helping explain unfamiliar terms, organize public evidence, draft analysis code, or prepare a clearer account of an investigation.

A good starting point is a small task with a defined method. Follow the project’s instructions, keep track of your sources and observations, and make your contribution understandable to the next person who reads it. You can build research skills while doing work that fits into a larger effort.

## How do citizen science and AI work together?

There are two useful ways to think about the connection. In one, volunteers help a research team develop or evaluate AI. In the other, participants use AI tools to assist their own research activities. A project can involve either approach, both, or neither.

Zooniverse’s [AI ethics framework](https://www.zooniverse.org/about/ai-ethics) gives concrete examples of the first approach: human classifications can help train models, and volunteers can check machine-generated classifications. The project team defines how human and machine work fit together.

For the second approach, an AI assistant might help you understand a dataset’s documentation or write code for a calculation. You remain responsible for following the research method and checking the output you contribute.

## Find a project that gives you a clear first task

If you want to contribute observations or classifications, start with an established project finder. [NASA’s citizen-science directory](https://science.nasa.gov/citizen-science/) lists projects with different equipment and participation requirements. Some involve a smartphone or laptop; others need a particular location or specialist equipment. [SciStarter](https://scistarter.org/) also lets you search for projects by topic and find online opportunities.

For shared research that asks contributors to bring their own AI tools, explore [LLM.Giving’s research library](/research). You can put unused AI usage toward a task with a clear output and completion criteria. Open a question, read its brief, and choose a ready task whose method you understand.

Pick a project where you can explain the purpose of your contribution in one sentence. “I am checking whether these sources cover the same time period” is a workable first objective.

## Use AI to learn the method before doing the work

Begin with the project’s own tutorial, protocol, or task prompt. If a term is unfamiliar, ask your AI to explain it using the supplied documentation. Then compare that explanation with the original material.

A useful learning prompt is:

> Explain the terms in these project instructions for a new volunteer. Separate the steps I must follow from background information. Point to the part of the supplied instructions that supports each required step, and list anything I should ask the research team to clarify.

This can help you prepare without changing the method. For a classification project, follow its rules on whether you may use outside AI tools while classifying data. The fact that a project uses machine learning does not automatically authorize a volunteer to replace a requested human judgment with an AI answer.

## Work through one bounded investigation

Consider a hypothetical shared-research question: which public datasets could help a community group compare local air-quality measurements? A first contribution could investigate one dataset and document whether it fits the group’s needs.

You could use AI to help locate the publisher’s documentation and extract a draft comparison. Your checks would include the pollutants measured, reporting units, station locations, observation period, and reuse conditions. For each entry, save the source and the relevant passage or page location.

When a detail is missing from the documentation, record it as an open question. A later contributor can contact the publisher, inspect the data, or look for another source.

## Keep observations, calculations, and interpretation distinct

A research note is easier to review when it separates three things:

- **What you observed:** the source passage, classification, measurement, or original data value.
- **What you did:** the selection rule, transformation, comparison, or calculation.
- **What you think it means:** the interpretation and its limits.

For example, a dataset’s documented collection dates are an observation from a source. Filtering it to a study period is a method. Deciding whether it is suitable for a comparison is an interpretation. Keeping these separate lets a reviewer challenge the decision without losing the underlying information.

Use AI to help structure the note, then check that it has not silently filled missing observations or changed a measurement. Preserve original values alongside any transformed version.

## Make review part of the contribution

Before submitting, reopen the important sources and check them against your final wording. For analysis code, test a small case whose expected result you can calculate separately. Record the inputs and units so another person can repeat the calculation.

Ask a fresh AI run to look for a specific class of error, such as unsupported citations or inconsistent units. Treat that as an additional check and inspect the issues it raises. When the project calls for an independent contributor’s review, follow that requirement as well.

On LLM.Giving, task results include evidence linked to completion criteria. Research feeds into synthesis and independent review, and corrections can reopen affected work. Read the current task instructions so you return the complete required result.

## What if the answer is inconclusive?

Report what you examined, what the evidence supports, and what remains unresolved. Explain which next observation, source, or analysis would help. For a defined investigation, finding that the available data cannot answer the question can prevent others from repeating the same attempt.

For example, you might discover that one dataset reports hourly measurements while another supplies daily averages for a different period. Document the mismatch and what would be needed for a valid comparison. Keep missing observations visible so later analysis can handle them explicitly.

## Start with a contribution you can explain

You do not need a PhD to begin learning a project’s method and contributing within its scope. Some tasks require specialist skills; others reward careful reading, consistent observations, and a willingness to ask precise questions.

Choose work you can explain in your own words. Keep private records out of public AI sessions and research submissions, use materials you have permission to share, and follow the receiving project’s rules.
